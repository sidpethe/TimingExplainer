Chapter 04

Why go optical?

Today's official second is defined by caesium atoms, probed with microwaves. Optical clocks use visible light instead, which oscillates tens of thousands of times faster.

Microwave clock · caesium-133
9.19 GHz

9.2 billion ticks per second

  • Defines the SI second since 1967
  • Best fountain clocks: ~1 part in 1016
  • Loses 1 second in ~300 million years
Optical clock · strontium-87
429 THz

429 trillion ticks per second · red light, 698 nm

  • ~47,000× more ticks per second than caesium
  • Best clocks: below 1 part in 1018
  • Would lose 1 second in ~40 billion years

The ruler analogy

Measuring time with a faster oscillator is like measuring length with a ruler that has finer markings. Both rulers below are the same length; one is just divided far more finely.

Coarse ruler — like a microwave clock

Fine ruler — like an optical clock

Same lengthMany more divisions → more precise readings

Physicists call this the quality factor: the clock frequency divided by the width of the resonance. Higher frequency plus a razor-thin resonance gives optical clocks a quality factor in the hundreds of trillions.

Chapter 05

Inside an optical lattice clock

An optical clock fills a laboratory table with lasers, mirrors and vacuum chambers. Its job comes down to four steps, repeated around once a second.

Step 1

Cool the atoms

A cloud of strontium atoms is hit from all sides by laser beams that act like a thick molasses, slowing them until they're just millionths of a degree above absolute zero.

≈ 0.000001 °C above −273.15 °C

Step 2

Trap them in light

The cold atoms are loaded into an "optical lattice": criss-crossing laser beams that form an egg-carton of light, holding thousands of atoms still so they can't drift or collide.

"Magic" wavelength: 813 nm

Step 3

Probe with an ultra-stable laser

A red "clock laser", stabilised against a vibration-isolated mirror cavity, shines on the atoms. A feedback loop keeps it tuned exactly to the atoms' resonance.

Linewidth: less than 1 Hz

Step 4

Count the ticks

No electronics can count 429 trillion cycles a second directly. A "frequency comb" — a laser producing evenly spaced colours like the teeth of a comb — acts as a gearbox, converting optical ticks into countable radio frequencies.

Nobel Prize in Physics, 2005

Lattice clocks

Use thousands of neutral atoms (strontium or ytterbium) at once. Measuring many atoms together averages out noise, so lattice clocks reach high precision quickly.

Trapped-ion clocks

Use a single charged atom (such as aluminium or ytterbium) held by electric fields. One isolated ion is exceptionally well shielded from disturbances.

Chapter 01

Why does a billionth of a second matter?

Modern life runs on synchronised time. Most of it is invisible — but if the world's clocks disagreed by even a few millionths of a second, many systems would quietly fail.

Navigation

GPS works by timing radio signals from satellites. Your position comes from how long those signals took to arrive.

1 ns error ≈ 30 cm

Telecoms & internet

Mobile networks hand your call between towers and pack data into precise time slots. Both need towers to agree on the time.

5G: ~1.5 µs alignment

Power grids

Grid operators time-stamp measurements across thousands of kilometres to keep alternating current in step and spot faults fast.

~1 µs time-stamps

Finance

Regulators require trades to be time-stamped accurately so the order of millions of transactions can be reconstructed.

EU rules: ≤100 µs

The GPS lesson

Each GPS satellite carries atomic clocks and continuously broadcasts the time. Your phone listens to several satellites and works out how far away each one is from the time delay of its signal.

Radio waves travel at the speed of light — about 30 centimetres every nanosecond. So every billionth of a second of clock error turns into roughly a third of a metre of position error.

Chapter 02

What actually is a clock?

Strip away the hands and the casing, and every clock ever made — from a grandfather clock to an optical atomic clock — has the same three ingredients.

An oscillator

Something that repeats regularly: a swinging pendulum, a vibrating quartz crystal, or a wave of light.

A counter

Something that counts the repetitions — gears, an electronic circuit, or, for optical clocks, a "frequency comb".

A reference

A way to check the oscillator stays true. In atomic clocks, the reference is nature itself: identical atoms.

Faster ticks, finer time

The faster your oscillator ticks, the more finely you can slice up time. A pendulum that swings once a second can't tell you much about a millisecond. A quartz watch ticks 32,768 times a second — much better.

Atomic clocks go further. The caesium clocks that define today's second tick over 9 billion times a second. Optical clocks tick around 50,000 times faster still.

A brief history

Five centuries of better ticking

  1. 1656

    The pendulum clock

    Christiaan Huygens builds the first pendulum clock, cutting errors from about 15 minutes a day to around 15 seconds.

  2. 1927

    Quartz arrives

    At Bell Labs, Warren Marrison and J.W. Horton build the first quartz clock, using a crystal that vibrates when electrified.

  3. 1955

    The first caesium clock

    Louis Essen and Jack Parry at the UK's National Physical Laboratory build the first practical caesium atomic clock.

  4. 1967

    The atomic second

    The second is officially redefined as 9,192,631,770 oscillations of the caesium-133 atom. Earth's rotation is no longer the master clock.

  5. 1997 & 2005

    Two Nobel breakthroughs

    Nobel Prizes for laser cooling of atoms (1997) and for the optical frequency comb (2005) supply the key tools for optical clocks.

  6. 2000s

    Optical clocks emerge

    Trapped-ion clocks and Hidetoshi Katori's "optical lattice" clocks begin to beat the best caesium standards.

  7. 2020s

    Beyond 10−18

    The best optical clocks reach uncertainties below one part in a billion billion, sensitive enough to feel gravity change over a millimetre.

  8. ~2030

    A new second?

    The international metrology community is preparing to redefine the second using optical clocks.

Chapter 03

Atoms: nature's perfect tuning forks

Pendulums wear out and quartz crystals age. But every strontium atom in the universe is identical, and that makes atoms the ultimate timing reference.

Electrons in an atom can only exist at certain energy levels, like rungs on a ladder. To push an electron from one rung up to the next, you must hit it with light of exactly the right frequency — not a little more, not a little less.

That frequency is fixed by the laws of physics. It doesn't change with age, manufacturer or location. So if you can tune a laser to precisely the frequency that makes atoms jump, the laser itself becomes your clock's pendulum.

In practice it's a game of "warmer, colder". Scientists shine the laser on the atoms, measure how many jumped, and nudge the laser's frequency to keep that number at its peak. This feedback loop runs continuously, steering the laser onto the atomic resonance.

A photon with exactly the right energy lifts the electron from the ground level to the excited level. Wrong frequency? Nothing happens.

Chapter 04

Why go optical?

Today's official second is defined by caesium atoms, probed with microwaves. Optical clocks use visible light instead, which oscillates tens of thousands of times faster.

The ruler analogy

Measuring time with a faster oscillator is like measuring length with a ruler that has finer markings. Both rulers below are the same length; one is just divided far more finely.

Physicists call this the quality factor: the clock frequency divided by the width of the resonance. Higher frequency plus a razor-thin resonance gives optical clocks a quality factor in the hundreds of trillions.

Chapter 05

Inside an optical lattice clock

An optical clock fills a laboratory table with lasers, mirrors and vacuum chambers. Its job comes down to four steps, repeated around once a second.

Step 1

Cool the atoms

A cloud of strontium atoms is hit from all sides by laser beams that act like a thick molasses, slowing them until they're just millionths of a degree above absolute zero.

≈ 0.000001 °C above −273.15 °C

Step 2

Trap them in light

The cold atoms are loaded into an "optical lattice": criss-crossing laser beams that form an egg-carton of light, holding thousands of atoms still so they can't drift or collide.

"Magic" wavelength: 813 nm

Step 3

Probe with an ultra-stable laser

A red "clock laser", stabilised against a vibration-isolated mirror cavity, shines on the atoms. A feedback loop keeps it tuned exactly to the atoms' resonance.

Linewidth: less than 1 Hz

Step 4

Count the ticks

No electronics can count 429 trillion cycles a second directly. A "frequency comb" — a laser producing evenly spaced colours like the teeth of a comb — acts as a gearbox, converting optical ticks into countable radio frequencies.

Nobel Prize in Physics, 2005

Lattice clocks

Use thousands of neutral atoms (strontium or ytterbium) at once. Measuring many atoms together averages out noise, so lattice clocks reach high precision quickly.

Trapped-ion clocks

Use a single charged atom (such as aluminium or ytterbium) held by electric fields. One isolated ion is exceptionally well shielded from disturbances.

Chapter 07

Clocks that can feel gravity

Einstein predicted that time runs slower closer to a massive object. Optical clocks are now so precise they can measure this effect when one clock sits just a centimetre higher than another.

According to general relativity, gravity slows time. A clock in your basement ticks very slightly slower than one in your attic. For everyday life the difference is absurdly small — but it's real, and it's measurable.

Raise a clock by one metre near Earth's surface and it runs faster by about one part in 1016. Caesium clocks can barely sense that. Optical clocks can detect height differences of a centimetre or less.

GPS has to account for this too: satellite clocks run about 38 microseconds per day fast compared with clocks on the ground (gravity and their orbital speed combined). Left uncorrected, GPS positions would drift by roughly 10 km every day.

This turns clocks into a new kind of instrument: "relativistic geodesy", measuring height and Earth's shape by comparing clock rates.

Chapter 08

What will we do with better clocks?

Every leap in timekeeping has unlocked technology nobody anticipated. Here's what optical clocks could bring.

Redefining the second

Optical clocks already outperform the caesium standard by around 100 times. Metrologists have set out a roadmap to redefine the SI second using optical transitions, with a decision targeted for around 2030. It would be the first change to the definition since 1967.

Mapping Earth

Clocks that sense centimetre height changes could monitor sea levels, volcanoes and groundwater by detecting tiny shifts in gravity.

Hunting dark matter

If invisible dark matter nudges the constants of nature, networks of clocks might catch it as tiny, correlated ticking changes.

Resilient navigation

Portable optical clocks on ships, aircraft and satellites could keep accurate time when GPS signals are jammed or unavailable.

Gravitational waves

Proposed space-based clock networks could detect ripples in spacetime at frequencies today's detectors can't reach.

Glossary

Key terms in plain English.

Frequency

How many times something repeats per second, measured in hertz (Hz). 1 THz (terahertz) is a trillion hertz.

Oscillator

Anything that repeats at a steady rate — the "pendulum" of a clock. In optical clocks, it's a laser locked to atoms.

Resonance

The specific frequency at which a system responds most strongly. Atoms only absorb light at their resonant frequencies.

Fractional uncertainty

How far a clock might be off relative to its frequency. 10⁻¹⁸ means an error of one part in a billion billion.

Laser cooling

Using carefully tuned laser light to slow atoms down, chilling them to within millionths of a degree of absolute zero.

Optical lattice

A grid of light formed by overlapping laser beams, which traps atoms in a regular pattern like eggs in a carton.

Magic wavelength

A lattice laser colour chosen so that trapping the atoms shifts both clock energy levels equally, leaving the ticking rate undisturbed.

Frequency comb

A laser whose light consists of thousands of evenly spaced colours. It links optical frequencies to countable radio frequencies.

Quality factor (Q)

Resonant frequency divided by resonance width. A higher Q means a sharper, more precisely defined tick.

Gravitational redshift

The slowing of time deeper in a gravitational field. Clocks lower down tick slower than clocks higher up.

Chapter 02

What actually is a clock?

Strip away the hands and the casing, and every clock ever made — from a grandfather clock to an optical atomic clock — has the same three ingredients.

An oscillator

Something that repeats regularly: a swinging pendulum, a vibrating quartz crystal, or a wave of light.

A counter

Something that counts the repetitions — gears, an electronic circuit, or, for optical clocks, a "frequency comb".

A reference

A way to check the oscillator stays true. In atomic clocks, the reference is nature itself: identical atoms.

Faster ticks, finer time

The faster your oscillator ticks, the more finely you can slice up time. A pendulum that swings once a second can't tell you much about a millisecond. A quartz watch ticks 32,768 times a second — much better.

Atomic clocks go further. The caesium clocks that define today's second tick over 9 billion times a second. Optical clocks tick around 50,000 times faster still.

A brief history

Five centuries of better ticking

  1. 1656

    The pendulum clock

    Christiaan Huygens builds the first pendulum clock, cutting errors from about 15 minutes a day to around 15 seconds.

  2. 1927

    Quartz arrives

    At Bell Labs, Warren Marrison and J.W. Horton build the first quartz clock, using a crystal that vibrates when electrified.

  3. 1955

    The first caesium clock

    Louis Essen and Jack Parry at the UK's National Physical Laboratory build the first practical caesium atomic clock.

  4. 1967

    The atomic second

    The second is officially redefined as 9,192,631,770 oscillations of the caesium-133 atom. Earth's rotation is no longer the master clock.

  5. 1997 & 2005

    Two Nobel breakthroughs

    Nobel Prizes for laser cooling of atoms (1997) and for the optical frequency comb (2005) supply the key tools for optical clocks.

  6. 2000s

    Optical clocks emerge

    Trapped-ion clocks and Hidetoshi Katori's "optical lattice" clocks begin to beat the best caesium standards.

  7. 2020s

    Beyond 10−18

    The best optical clocks reach uncertainties below one part in a billion billion, sensitive enough to feel gravity change over a millimetre.

  8. ~2030

    A new second?

    The international metrology community is preparing to redefine the second using optical clocks.

Chapter 03

Atoms: nature's perfect tuning forks

Pendulums wear out and quartz crystals age. But every strontium atom in the universe is identical, and that makes atoms the ultimate timing reference.

Electrons in an atom can only exist at certain energy levels, like rungs on a ladder. To push an electron from one rung up to the next, you must hit it with light of exactly the right frequency — not a little more, not a little less.

That frequency is fixed by the laws of physics. It doesn't change with age, manufacturer or location. So if you can tune a laser to precisely the frequency that makes atoms jump, the laser itself becomes your clock's pendulum.

In practice it's a game of "warmer, colder". Scientists shine the laser on the atoms, measure how many jumped, and nudge the laser's frequency to keep that number at its peak. This feedback loop runs continuously, steering the laser onto the atomic resonance.

A photon with exactly the right energy lifts the electron from the ground level to the excited level. Wrong frequency? Nothing happens.

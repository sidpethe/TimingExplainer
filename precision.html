Chapter 07

Clocks that can feel gravity

Einstein predicted that time runs slower closer to a massive object. Optical clocks are now so precise they can measure this effect when one clock sits just a centimetre higher than another.

According to general relativity, gravity slows time. A clock in your basement ticks very slightly slower than one in your attic. For everyday life the difference is absurdly small — but it's real, and it's measurable.

Raise a clock by one metre near Earth's surface and it runs faster by about one part in 1016. Caesium clocks can barely sense that. Optical clocks can detect height differences of a centimetre or less.

GPS has to account for this too: satellite clocks run about 38 microseconds per day fast compared with clocks on the ground (gravity and their orbital speed combined). Left uncorrected, GPS positions would drift by roughly 10 km every day.

This turns clocks into a new kind of instrument: "relativistic geodesy", measuring height and Earth's shape by comparing clock rates.

Chapter 08

What will we do with better clocks?

Every leap in timekeeping has unlocked technology nobody anticipated. Here's what optical clocks could bring.

Redefining the second

Optical clocks already outperform the caesium standard by around 100 times. Metrologists have set out a roadmap to redefine the SI second using optical transitions, with a decision targeted for around 2030. It would be the first change to the definition since 1967.

Mapping Earth

Clocks that sense centimetre height changes could monitor sea levels, volcanoes and groundwater by detecting tiny shifts in gravity.

Hunting dark matter

If invisible dark matter nudges the constants of nature, networks of clocks might catch it as tiny, correlated ticking changes.

Resilient navigation

Portable optical clocks on ships, aircraft and satellites could keep accurate time when GPS signals are jammed or unavailable.

Gravitational waves

Proposed space-based clock networks could detect ripples in spacetime at frequencies today's detectors can't reach.

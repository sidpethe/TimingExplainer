Chapter 09

Why 5G towers share a heartbeat

Most 5G networks talk and listen on the same radio channel, taking turns thousands of times a second. That only works if every tower switches at the same moment as its neighbours.

This scheme is called time-division duplexing (TDD). For a fraction of a millisecond every tower transmits to phones (the downlink). Then, after a tiny pause called the guard period, every tower falls silent and listens for the much fainter signals coming back from phones (the uplink).

A tower's transmitter is enormously louder than a phone. If one tower is still transmitting when its neighbour has started listening, the neighbour hears a roar instead of a whisper, and the phones it serves are effectively cut off.

So the 5G standards require cells that overlap to agree on the time to within 3 microseconds. In practice, operators keep every tower within ±1.5 µs of UTC, and today that time almost always comes from GPS and other satellite systems (GNSS).

Satellite timing signals are faint by the time they reach the ground. They can be jammed, spoofed, disrupted by space weather, or lost through a simple antenna fault. When that happens, each tower falls back on its own local "holdover" clock.

Handing a moving phone from one tower to the next relies on both towers sharing a clock. Out of step, handovers fail and calls drop.

Features that combine signals from several antennas or towers need even tighter timing, so they are the first to be switched off.

5G can locate phones, including emergency callers, by timing signals from several towers. Every nanosecond of disagreement adds error.

    The fix

    How portable optical clocks help

    Optical clocks used to fill a laboratory. New designs fit in a few rack units: small enough for a telephone exchange, a data centre or a ship.

    One clock, a whole region

    You don't need an optical clock on every mast. An operator can place one at a regional hub and share its time with dozens of towers over the fibre links that already carry their data, using protocols such as PTP (IEEE 1588) or White Rabbit.

    While GPS is healthy, the optical clock quietly checks it. When GPS is jammed, the towers carry on with the hub's time. Because the optical clock drifts by only around a nanosecond a day, the whole region stays inside its ±1.5 µs budget for months or longer.

    It also works as a lie detector: if GPS suddenly disagrees with a clock that can't drift that fast, the signal is probably being spoofed.

    Its pace comes from atoms, not satellites

    A quartz crystal's rate has to be corrected by GPS all the time, so once GPS disappears it starts to wander. An optical clock's rate is fixed by the atoms or molecules inside it, so it keeps its own pace with no outside help. That is exactly what you want in a backup.

    Months, not hours

    Drifting about 1 ns a day, an optical clock would take years to use up 5G's timing budget. Quartz uses it up in hours.

    A spoofing alarm

    A fake GPS signal that tries to drag the time away gets caught, because the optical clock simply disagrees with it.

    Built to travel

    Ruggedised, rack-mounted optical clocks are already being trialled at sea, in data centres and on telecom fibre networks.

    Beyond 5G

    The same backup protects power grids, financial time-stamps and navigation whenever satellites are unavailable.

    ~4 yrs

    At about 1 nanosecond of drift per day, a portable optical clock would take roughly four years to use up 5G's ±1.5 µs budget. Typical quartz backups use it up in hours.
